# Impact assessment

Outcome: a reasoned assessment of why the contribution matters, to whom, what supports that value, what limits it, and concrete ways to strengthen it. Kept in `research/impact.md` when the researcher asks to keep it.

## Step 1: Establish the contribution

1. Identify what is assessed: an idea note, the research question in `requirements/project.md`, a result record in `research/results/`, or the manuscript's main claim. Read these, their linked evidence notes, and `research/impact.md` if it exists.
2. State the contribution in one or two sentences and name where it comes from: the researcher's words or a file. If the contribution is unclear, say so and offer `idea-deepening`. Never assess a contribution that you had to invent.

## Step 2: Examine the value

3. **Audiences.** Which communities, practitioners, or decisions the contribution could inform. Ground each audience in sources or the researcher's statement; label anything else as an assumption.
4. **Kind of value.** For example, it resolves a contradiction, explains an observation, extends a method, provides data, corrects an earlier result, or offers an interpretation. Fit the kind of value to the discipline.
5. **Support.** What inspected evidence shows that the problem matters, with locators (use `literature-and-evidence`); what the project's own results show (`research/results/`, with their status); and what is still assumed. Keep inspected full text, abstracts, and search snippets distinct.
6. **Limits.** Scope, generalizability, competing or overlapping work, dependence on results that do not exist yet, and alternative explanations.
7. Keep impact apart from novelty (`idea-deepening`), venue fit (`journal-selection`), and readiness of a manuscript (`submission-readiness`). A journal's prestige or metric does not establish the value of this paper. Never invent a citation count, an adoption, an audience size, a policy effect, or an acceptance probability.

## Step 3: Strengthen

8. Give concrete improvements, for example a comparison to add, a boundary to state, an audience to address, evidence to obtain, or a claim to narrow. For each, say what it needs: new research, new sources, or only writing.
   Help express the contribution persuasively. Emphatic value judgments and ambitious statements of potential are allowed; do not flatten them into cautious prose merely because they are promotional. Judge whether the wording implies a new factual result, an unperformed comparison, a broader validated scope, or an already-demonstrated practical effect. Bring such substantive changes back to the researcher with the specific evidence issue; ordinary impact rhetoric remains an authorial choice.
9. Recommend one improvement and ask what the researcher wants to do. A change to the research question or the main claim is a major decision; after a clear yes, record it with `constraint-maintenance`.

## Step 4: Record when asked

10. Keep the assessment only when the researcher asks. Add a dated section to `research/impact.md` in the interaction language: the contribution assessed (with a link), audiences, kind of value, support with links, limits, improvements, and which statements are assumptions. Link the section from the idea note when there is one. Keep earlier sections unless the researcher asks to replace them.
11. An assessment is neither evidence nor a requirement. Do not copy its conclusions into the manuscript as findings.

## Completion check

- The contribution assessed is the researcher's, with its source named.
- Each audience and kind of value is supported by an inspected source or a result, or is labeled as an assumption.
- Impact is kept apart from novelty, venue fit, and readiness; there is no score, invented number, or acceptance prediction.
- The improvements are concrete and say what each needs.
- Nothing was saved unless the researcher asked.
