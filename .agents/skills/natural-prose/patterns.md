# Pattern Families

Language-independent patterns that make prose read as formulaic, inflated, or machine-produced, and how to handle each one in scholarly text. The lists for [English](patterns-en.md), [Chinese](patterns-zh.md), and [Japanese](patterns-ja.md) give the concrete signals and examples of every family.

## How to use the families

Most of these patterns share one cause: wording chosen because it would fit almost any text, not because it fits this one. Each family gives the signals, why they weaken scholarly prose, what to do, and when to leave the text alone.

- A **strong** pattern warrants attention even when it occurs only once; a style revision must still preserve meaning, and a substantive claim problem goes to the researcher.
- A **weak** pattern is common in careful human writing too. Act on it only when several patterns cluster in one passage, or when the same one repeats through the text.
- A confirmed writing rule, a venue requirement, or the researcher's own habit outranks every pattern.
- A pattern is a reason to look, never evidence about who wrote the text.
- Apply the skill's content-preservation rule to every family and example. Removing repetition or conversational framing must keep every distinct claim, comparison, degree, attribution, and limit. When a concrete factual claim has no support, retain it visibly in a `[TODO: ...]` marker for verification or the researcher's decision. Conventional emphasis, evaluative contribution statements, and future prospects do not need a marker merely because they are ambitious; inspect what the wording actually asserts.

## 1. Emphasis without content (strong)

- **Signals.** A contrast with a position that nobody holds ("not merely X, but Y"). A closing sentence that repeats the paragraph or declares it important. A saying in place of the point. A sentence that announces what comes next instead of saying it. An answer to an objection that the text never raised.
- **Why it weakens the text.** The sentence asks the reader to treat a point as important without adding information, and reviewers read it as padding.
- **Do.** State the point directly. Cut the repetition. Swap the saying for the concrete claim that the text can support.
- **Keep.** A contrast that corrects a view the reader or the cited literature actually holds. A final sentence that adds a consequence. A short roadmap at the end of an introduction when the field or the venue expects one.

## 2. Impact language and overstatement (context-dependent)

- **Signals.** Pivotal, transformative, groundbreaking, "paves the way", and "holds great promise" invite a context check, not an automatic objection. A claim to be the first, universal superiority, a demonstrated practical benefit, or a statistically significant difference asserts something more concrete.
- **Why it can weaken the text.** Repeated generic praise can obscure the contribution. An assertion that changes the findings, comparison scope, validation status, or demonstrated effect can misstate the evidence. The strength of the adjective alone does not establish that problem.
- **Do.** Preserve the author's intended emphasis and make the contribution concrete where the original permits. Separate an evaluative judgment or outlook from a claim of an observed benefit. Flag a specific factual mismatch with its evidence; do not demand a citation or statistical test merely for promotional tone. Interpret "significant" in context: statistical significance needs the reported analysis, while qualitative importance or a large measured difference does not automatically assert a statistical test. Narrowing or dropping a substantive claim requires the researcher's decision.
- **Keep.** Author-chosen emphasis, a persuasive contribution statement, and an ambitious outlook, including conventional impact rhetoric, when they do not turn an unperformed comparison, unobserved result, or untested application into an established fact.

## 3. Unsourced authority and vague relations (strong when uncited)

- **Signals.** "Studies show", "experts agree", "it is widely accepted" without a citation. "Associated with", "linked to", or "related to" where the text knows the actual relation.
- **Why it weakens the text.** In scholarly writing such a claim needs its source. A vague relation can hide a causal claim that the evidence cannot carry, or understate one that it can.
- **Do.** Cite the source whose support was checked; otherwise keep the claim, including its attribution and stated strength, with a `[TODO: ...]` marker. Make a relation more specific only when the original already states or directly entails it. New evidence that would change the relation is a substantive issue for the researcher, not a style repair.
- **Keep.** "Associated with" when an association is exactly what was measured.

## 4. Template rhythm (weak)

- **Signals.** Items in threes whatever their real number. A connective at the start of nearly every sentence. Consecutive sentences with the same opening. Sentences of uniform length. Dashes as the default joint between clauses. Several hedges on one claim.
- **Why it weakens the text.** Rhythm applied by rule hides the logic. Stacked hedges blur the actual degree of certainty.
- **Do.** List as many items as exist. Let sentence order carry the logic, and keep the connectives that state a real relation (contrast, cause, sequence). Merge or vary repeated openings. Choose the punctuation that says how two clauses relate. Reduce stacked hedges only when the remaining wording preserves the original uncertainty and every separate scope or degree limit.
- **Keep.** A real three-part structure, deliberate parallelism, a connective that marks a real turn, and dashes that the researcher uses or the venue's style requires.

## 5. Wordy constructions (weak)

- **Signals.** A long phrase where a plain verb exists: "serves as" for "is", "boasts" for "has", "perform an analysis of" for "analyze", and the verbose frames listed for each language.
- **Why it weakens the text.** The extra words carry no meaning and slow the reader.
- **Do.** Use the plain verb.
- **Keep.** A construction that the field uses as a term of art, and one that carries aspect or tense that the short form would lose.

## 6. Stock vocabulary (weak; strong in clusters)

- **Signals.** Words that generated text uses far more often than human authors of the same genre; each language list names them.
- **Why it weakens the text.** In clusters they make the text generic; one of them alone is an ordinary word.
- **Do.** Use the plain, specific word. Often the sentence improves when the word is simply deleted.
- **Keep.** Technical senses ("robust estimator", "closed-loop control"), quotations, and terms that the researcher defined.

## 7. Template formatting (strong in running text)

- **Signals.** Bold or italics for emphasis inside sentences. List items that each open with a bolded heading word and a colon. Bullet points where the argument needs connected prose. Headings in a capitalization style that the venue does not use. Emojis, arrows, and decorative rules.
- **Why it weakens the text.** Scholarly prose carries emphasis through sentence structure, and a list cuts the reasoning that connects its points.
- **Do.** Follow the workspace conventions and the venue's output profile. When the points of such a list depend on each other, write them as connected prose.
- **Keep.** Lists of genuinely separate items, such as the steps of a procedure, inclusion criteria, or hypotheses, and any formatting that the venue or the researcher requires.

## 8. Assistant residue and placeholders (strong; remove)

- **Signals.** Phrases addressed to a user ("Certainly", "Here is the revised version", "I hope this helps"). Remarks about a model's knowledge or its cutoff date. Sentences about the text itself ("This revision now emphasizes"). Placeholders such as "[insert citation]" or "XX%".
- **Why it weakens the text.** None of it belongs in a manuscript or a letter, and it is the clearest sign of unedited generated text.
- **Do.** Delete the conversational framing; the substance it carried stays. Turn a placeholder into a `[TODO: ...]` marker that names what is missing; never fill it with a guess. An AI signature or attribution never stays in manuscript text.
- **Keep.** A disclosure statement that the researcher decided to include under the venue's policy.

## 9. Translated-sounding prose (weak)

- **Signals.** Structures carried over from another language: long chains of modifiers before a noun, stacked passives, explicit subjects that the language would drop, loanwords where an ordinary word exists, and the punctuation of the other language.
- **Why it weakens the text.** Native readers of the manuscript language read it slowly and may misread it.
- **Do.** Rebuild the sentence in the natural order of the manuscript language. Split long modifier chains. Keep the established technical terms.
- **Keep.** Terms of art and the field's settled wording, even when they are loanwords, and a passive whose agent is unknown or irrelevant.

## 10. Letters that bury the answer (letters and replies)

- **Signals.** A response that restates the reviewer's comment at length, explains background that the reviewer knows, and names the actual change last. The same elaborate thanks before every answer.
- **Why it weakens the text.** Editors and reviewers look for what changed and where.
- **Do.** Lead with the action and its location in the revised manuscript. Include just enough reasoning for the reviewer to accept the change.
- **Keep.** The courtesy conventions of the field and the venue, including a short thank-you.

## 11. Excessive caution and understatement (context-dependent)

- **Signals.** A checked result described as a mere possibility; several qualifiers attached to the same inference; generic warnings repeated across sections; an unpromised task framed as a failure; a conclusion that lists absences without stating the supported contribution.
- **Why it weakens the text.** It can misrepresent the evidence in the weaker direction and hide what readers can use or cite. A hedge count does not establish the problem.
- **Do.** Check inspected evidence before changing strength. Keep uncertainty and scope in a style edit. State checked findings directly and explain the contribution and its value. For a requested claim-strength or venue-impact assessment, follow [argument and impact guidance](../manuscript-writing/argument-and-impact.md). Keep empirical, causal, novelty, generalization, and validation claims supported; connect material limitations to the claims they affect.
- **Keep.** Genuine uncertainty, unfavorable or null results, limits that affect a claim or reasonable use, venue-required sections, and researcher-authored wording unless the request authorizes the change. Ambitious prospects can stay as prospects.

## When not to act

- Quotations, titles of works, proper names, and defined terms stay as they are, even when they contain a listed word.
- A sentence that discusses a phrase, rather than using it, stays.
- Wording that a confirmed rule, a venue requirement, or text agreed with coauthors fixes stays unless the researcher asks.
- When a pattern may be deliberate, ask the researcher instead of flattening their style.
